## Overview

A corneal ulcer is a serious eye condition involving an open wound or erosion on the cornea, the clear front surface of the eye. While small corneal scratches may heal on their own, true ulcers involve loss of corneal tissue and carry significant risk of scarring and vision loss if not treated promptly.

Corneal ulcers demand urgent evaluation and treatment because they can progress rapidly, potentially causing permanent damage within days.

---

## Types & Causes

### Infectious Ulcers
- **Bacterial:** Most common; often associated with contact lens wear
- **Viral:** Herpes simplex virus (HSV); recurrent and difficult to treat
- **Fungal:** Rare but serious; often after vegetative trauma
- **Parasitic:** Uncommon; associated with contact lens contamination

### Non-Infectious Ulcers
- **Traumatic:** Abrasion that becomes infected
- **Chemical injury:** Alkali or acid exposure
- **Inflammatory:** Rheumatoid arthritis, Sjögren's syndrome
- **Neurotropic:** Reduced corneal sensation

---

## Symptoms

### Early Signs
- **Eye pain:** Sharp, constant, worse with blinking
- **Foreign body sensation:** Feeling like something is in the eye
- **Redness:** Surrounding white part of eye
- **Tearing:** Excessive watering

### Advanced Signs
- **Decreased vision:** Blurring or haziness
- **Discharge:** Yellow, green, or purulent drainage
- **Photophobia:** Sensitivity to light
- **Eyelid swelling:** Edema and inflammation

---

## Risk Factors

- **Contact lens wear:** Especially with poor hygiene or overnight wear
- **Eye trauma:** Scratches, foreign bodies, chemical exposure
- **Dry eye disease:** Reduced tear film stability
- **Reduced blinking:** Prolonged screen time, Bell's palsy
- **Immunosuppression:** HIV, diabetes, chemotherapy
- **Ocular surface disease:** Stevens-Johnson syndrome, graft-versus-host disease

---

## Diagnosis

### Clinical Examination
- **Visual acuity:** Assess severity of vision impact
- **Slit lamp microscopy:** Visualize ulcer size, depth, location
- **Fluorescein staining:** Ulcer edges highlighted in green
- **Conjunctival swab:** Culture to identify organism (bacterial, fungal, viral)

### Imaging (if needed)
- **Corneal topography:** Map ulcer and surrounding irregularities
- **B-scan ultrasound:** Assess posterior involvement (rare)

---

## Treatment Options

### Immediate Care (First 24-48 Hours)
- **Topical antibiotics:** Fortified hourly drops (gatifloxacin, cefazolin)
- **Antifungal drops:** If fungal suspected (natamycin, voriconazole)
- **Antiviral drops:** If HSV suspected (acyclovir, trifluridine)
- **Cycloplegic agents:** Ease pain, reduce inflammation (cyclopentolate)
- **Lubricating drops:** Support healing
- **Eye patch or bandage contact lens:** Protect and comfort

### Medical Management (Ongoing)
- **Topical fortified antibiotics:** Tapered as ulcer improves
- **Topical steroids:** Used cautiously to reduce inflammation once infection controlled
- **Oral antivirals:** For HSV ulcers (acyclovir, valacyclovir)
- **Oral antifungals:** For fungal ulcers
- **Pain management:** Oral analgesia if severe

### Surgical Intervention (if needed)
- **Debridement:** Removal of necrotic tissue
- **Conjunctival flap:** Cover ulcer to promote healing
- **Corneal transplant:** For large, vision-threatening ulcers with scarring

| **4-6 weeks** | Late healing | Most small ulcers healed; transition to maintenance drops |
| **2-3 months** | Remodeling | Corneal haze may persist; refractive changes possible |

---

## Complications (If Untreated)

- **Corneal scarring:** Permanent vision reduction
- **Keratectasia:** Corneal bulging and weakening
- **Perforation:** Full-thickness corneal hole (surgical emergency)
- **Secondary glaucoma:** Inflammation-induced IOP elevation
- **Phthisis bulbi:** End-stage shrunken, blind eye

---

## Prevention

### Contact Lens Safety
- Follow proper cleaning and storage protocols
- Never sleep in contact lenses (unless approved extended wear)
- Replace lenses on recommended schedule
- Avoid tap water contact with lenses

### General Eye Safety
- Protect eyes during activities (sports, construction)
- Seek prompt treatment for minor scratches
- Maintain good eyelid hygiene

### Systemic Management
- Control diabetes (reduces infection risk)
- Manage autoimmune conditions
- Avoid immunosuppressive medications if possible
