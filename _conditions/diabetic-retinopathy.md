## Overview

Diabetic retinopathy is a serious eye condition caused by high blood sugar damaging the blood vessels in the retina (the light-sensitive tissue at the back of the eye). It is the leading cause of vision loss and blindness in working-age adults in developed countries.

Early stages often cause no symptoms, making regular eye exams essential for all people with diabetes, regardless of blood sugar control or presence of symptoms.

---

## Risk Factors & Prevention

### Modifiable Risk Factors
- **Poor blood sugar control:** HbA1c > 8% significantly increases risk
- **High blood pressure:** Hypertension accelerates retinal damage
- **High cholesterol:** Dyslipidemia worsens progression
- **Smoking:** Increases risk 2-3 fold
- **Poor diet:** High glycemic index foods worsen control

### Non-Modifiable Factors
- **Diabetes duration:** Longer duration = higher risk
- **Type of diabetes:** Type 1 generally progresses faster than Type 2
- **Genetics:** Family history of diabetic retinopathy

### Prevention Strategy
**Target:** Blood sugar (HbA1c < 7%), Blood pressure (< 130/80), Cholesterol (LDL < 70 if on statin)

## Stages of Diabetic Retinopathy

### Stage 1: Nonproliferative (Early)
- Microaneurysms (tiny outpouchings in vessels)
- Retinal hemorrhages (dot and blot)
- Hard exudates (lipid deposits)
- **Vision:** Usually normal at this stage
- **Prognosis:** Reversible with good glycemic control

### Stage 2: Nonproliferative (Moderate to Severe)
- Venous beading (sausage-like veins)
- Intraretinal microvascular abnormalities (IRMA)
- Cotton-wool spots (nerve fiber layer infarcts)
- **Vision:** May be unaffected; Macular edema may develop
- **Prognosis:** Risk of progression; needs treatment if macular edema present

### Stage 3: Proliferative
- Neovascularization (abnormal new vessels)
- Vitreous hemorrhage (bleeding into gel)
- Tractional retinal detachment
- **Vision:** Significant vision loss possible
- **Prognosis:** Highest risk of blindness; requires urgent treatment

### Stage 4: Diabetic Macular Edema (DME)
- Fluid accumulation in central retina (macula)
- Can occur at any stage of retinopathy
- **Vision:** Blurred/distorted central vision
- **Prognosis:** Reversible with early treatment; progressive if untreated

## Symptoms

### Early Stages (Often Asymptomatic)
- No noticeable vision changes
- Detected only on eye exam
- **Importance:** Regular screening essential

### Mid-to-Advanced Stages
- Floaters (spots/lines in vision)
- Blurred or fluctuating vision
- Difficulty reading or recognizing faces
- Dark or blank spots in central vision
- Flashing lights (vitreous hemorrhage)

### Vision-Threatening (Emergency)
- Sudden floaters and flashing lights
- Sudden significant vision loss
- **Action:** Seek immediate evaluation at 24/7 trauma center

## Diagnosis

### Comprehensive Dilated Exam
- **Direct ophthalmoscopy:** View retinal changes
- **Indirect ophthalmoscopy:** 360-degree retinal assessment
- **Slit lamp biomicroscopy:** Detailed anterior and posterior segment

### Imaging Studies
- **Retinal photography:** Document hemorrhages, exudates, vessels
- **Optical Coherence Tomography (OCT):** Measure macular thickness; detect edema
- **Fluorescein angiography (FA):** Visualize leaking vessels and nonperfused areas
- **Fundus autofluorescence (FAF):** Identify areas of retinal damage

## Treatment Options

### Nonproliferative Diabetic Retinopathy (NPDR)

**No Treatment Required IF:**
- No macular edema present
- Vision is normal
- Blood sugar and BP well controlled

**Treatment IF:**
- Macular edema develops (see DME below)
- Progression to proliferative stage appears imminent

### Diabetic Macular Edema (DME)

**First-Line: Anti-VEGF Injections**
- Monthly injections reduce edema and restore vision in 60-70% of patients
- Options: Bevacizumab, Ranibizumab, Aflibercept
- Better outcomes for recent-onset edema

**Second-Line: Laser Photocoagulation**
- Grid or focal laser burns reduce leakage
- Used when anti-VEGF ineffective
- May cause peripheral vision loss

**Third-Line: Intravitreal Steroids**
- Triamcinolone or dexamethasone implants
- Reserved for sterile DME or failed anti-VEGF therapy

### Proliferative Diabetic Retinopathy (PDR)

**Pan-Retinal Photocoagulation (PRP)**
- Laser burns 1000-1500 areas of peripheral retina
- Reduces oxygen demand, prevents neovascularization growth
- Success rate: 50-60% prevent severe vision loss

**Anti-VEGF Injections**
- Can be used adjunctively with PRP
- May allow lower laser doses (less peripheral vision loss)

**Vitrectomy Surgery**
- For extensive vitreous hemorrhage
- For tractional retinal detachment
- Allows direct visualization and membrane peeling

## Complications (If Untreated)

- **Severe vision loss or blindness:** Permanent without treatment
- **Vitreous hemorrhage:** Requires surgical clearing
- **Retinal detachment:** Requires vitrectomy and retinal repair
- **Neovascular glaucoma:** High intraocular pressure; difficult to manage
- **Phthisis bulbi:** End-stage atrophic eye

---

## Monitoring & Prevention

### Eye Exam Frequency
- **No DR:** Annually
- **Mild NPDR:** Every 6-12 months
- **Moderate NPDR:** Every 3-6 months
- **Severe NPDR or PDR:** Every 2-4 weeks
- **DME:** Monthly initially

### Systemic Management
- **Diabetes control:** Target HbA1c < 7% (< 6.5% if new-onset)
- **Blood pressure:** < 130/80 mmHg
- **Lipids:** LDL < 70 mg/dL (on statin)
- **Aspirin:** 81-325 mg daily for primary prevention if indicated
- **Kidney function:** Proteinuria increases risk; manage ACE-I/ARB
