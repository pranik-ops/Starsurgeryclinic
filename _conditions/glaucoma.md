## Overview

Glaucoma is a group of eye diseases characterized by optic nerve damage, usually caused by elevated intraocular pressure (IOP). It is a leading cause of irreversible blindness worldwide, affecting over 76 million people.

The condition often progresses silently without symptoms until significant vision loss has occurred, making regular screening critical for early detection and treatment.

---

## Types of Glaucoma

### Primary Open-Angle Glaucoma (POAG)
- **Prevalence:** 60-70% of all glaucoma cases
- **Mechanism:** Gradual increase in IOP due to impaired aqueous outflow
- **Progression:** Slow, typically over years to decades
- **Risk:** High in older adults, African Americans, those with family history
- **Screening:** Important for all adults over 40

### Angle-Closure Glaucoma
- **Prevalence:** 5-10% of glaucoma cases
- **Mechanism:** Iris blocks drainage angle; rapid IOP elevation
- **Presentation:** Acute angle-closure is medical emergency (severe pain, vision loss)
- **Risk:** Higher in hyperopic, older women, Asian descent
- **Prevention:** Laser iridotomy for anatomically narrow angles

### Normal-Tension Glaucoma
- **Prevalence:** 20-30% of open-angle cases
- **Characteristic:** Optic nerve damage despite normal IOP (< 21 mmHg)
- **Mechanism:** Vascular insufficiency or inherent optic nerve weakness
- **Treatment:** Still requires IOP lowering (target 30% reduction)

### Secondary Glaucomas
- **Post-traumatic glaucoma:** After eye injury
- **Neovascular glaucoma:** Abnormal vessel growth (diabetes, retinal vein occlusion)
- **Inflammatory glaucoma:** Associated with uveitis
- **Steroid-induced glaucoma:** From topical/systemic steroids

## Risk Factors

### Primary Risk Factors
- **Elevated intraocular pressure:** IOP > 21 mmHg
- **Age:** Risk increases significantly after 60
- **Family history:** 5-10 times higher risk if parent affected
- **African descent:** 4-8 times higher risk; earlier onset

### Secondary Risk Factors
- **Thin corneas:** Central corneal thickness < 555 microns
- **High myopia:** Myopic refractive error
- **Diabetes:** Doubles glaucoma risk
- **Hypertension:** Increases IOP variability
- **Sleep apnea:** Associated with normal-tension glaucoma
- **Vascular disease:** Cardiovascular risk increases glaucoma risk

## Symptoms

### Open-Angle Glaucoma
- **Early/Moderate:** Usually asymptomatic (silent blindness)
- **Advanced:** Peripheral vision loss (noticed too late often)
- **Detection:** Requires professional screening; patient rarely notices early changes

### Acute Angle-Closure Glaucoma (MEDICAL EMERGENCY)
- **Severe eye pain**
- **Blurred vision with halos around lights**
- **Red eye with corneal edema**
- **Nausea and vomiting**
- **Fixed, dilated pupil**
- **Action:** Seek immediate emergency care; irreversible blindness develops within 24-48 hours if untreated

## Diagnosis

### Intraocular Pressure (IOP) Measurement
- **Applanation tonometry:** Gold standard (Goldmann, Tonopen)
- **Normal range:** 10-21 mmHg
- **Elevated:** > 21 mmHg (but not all elevated IOP = glaucoma)

### Optic Nerve Assessment
- **Direct/Indirect ophthalmoscopy:** Visualize optic disc
- **Cup-to-disc ratio:** Elevated if > 0.5 (suggests cupping)
- **Optic nerve photography:** Baseline for comparison

### Optic Nerve Imaging
- **Optical Coherence Tomography (OCT):** Quantifies optic nerve fiber layer (RNFL) thickness
- **Confocal Scanning Laser Ophthalmoscopy (HRT):** 3D imaging of optic nerve
- **Scanning Laser Polarimetry (GDx):** Polarization-based RNFL measurement

### Visual Field Testing
- **Automated perimetry:** Gold standard for detecting glaucomatous damage
- **Baseline:** Critical for future comparison
- **Frequency:** Yearly or more often if abnormal

### Gonioscopy
- **Direct visualization of drainage angle**
- **Determines open vs. closed angle**
- **Risk stratification**

## Treatment Goals

**Primary objective:** Lower IOP enough to halt optic nerve damage and prevent vision loss

**Target IOP:** Individualized based on:
- Severity at diagnosis (lower target if advanced)
- Risk factors (lower target if high-risk)
- Baseline IOP and rate of progression
- Patient age and life expectancy

---

## Treatment Options

### Medical Management (First-Line)
- **Prostaglandin analogs:** Latanoprost, timolol (most effective single agent)
- **Beta-blockers:** Timolol, betaxolol
- **Alpha-agonists:** Brimonidine, apraclonidine
- **Carbonic anhydrase inhibitors:** Dorzolamide, brinzolamide
- **Rho kinase inhibitors:** Netarsudil (newer agent)

### Laser Treatment
- **Selective Laser Trabeculoplasty (SLT):** Reduces IOP 20-30%; repeatable
- **Argon Laser Trabeculoplasty (ALT):** Older technique; 50% effective
- **Laser Peripheral Iridotomy:** For angle-closure prevention/treatment

### Surgical Treatment
- **Trabeculectomy:** Gold standard; 60-80% achieve target IOP
- **Glaucoma Drainage Devices:** For severe/refractory glaucoma
- **Cyclosurgery:** Reduces aqueous production
- **Tube Shunts:** For complicated glaucoma

## Complications (If Untreated)

- **Progressive peripheral vision loss**
- **Central vision loss (late stage)**
- **Irreversible blindness**
- **Acute angle-closure:** Can cause blindness within 24-48 hours

---

## Monitoring & Long-Term Care

### Appointment Frequency
- **New diagnosis:** Every 2-4 weeks initially
- **Stabilized:** Every 3-6 months
- **Advanced/high-risk:** Every 1-3 months

### Self-Monitoring
- **Adhere to medications:** Critical for success
- **Report any vision changes:** Pain, halos, blur warrant urgent evaluation
- **Track side effects:** Report to ophthalmologist

### Lifestyle Recommendations
- **Avoid Valsalva maneuvers:** Don't strain; can transiently raise IOP
- **Maintain healthy weight:** Obesity associated with higher IOP
- **Exercise regularly:** Modest aerobic exercise may lower IOP
- **Limit caffeine:** High caffeine consumption may raise IOP

---

## Prevention & Early Detection

### Screening Recommendations
- **Age 40-60:** At least every 3-5 years
- **Age > 60:** Every 1-2 years
- **Family history of glaucoma:** Annually from age 20
- **African descent:** Annually from age 20 (higher risk)

### Who Should Get Screened
- Everyone age 40+
- Anyone with family history
- Those with elevated IOP
- Diabetics
