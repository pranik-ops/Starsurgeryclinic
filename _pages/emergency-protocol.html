Immediate First-Aid Protocols

Follow these protocols immediately upon eye injury. Time is critical in ocular trauma.

🧪 Chemical Burns (Acid/Alkali)

IMMEDIATE ACTION REQUIRED
  1. Irrigate immediately with clean tap water or saline solution
  2. Duration: 15–20 minutes of continuous irrigation
  3. Method: Gently flush from inner corner (nose side) to outer corner
  4. DO NOT: Rub the eye or apply any medications first
  5. Transport: Proceed directly to our emergency room while continuing irrigation if possible

⚠️ Tip: Even after irrigation, chemical burns require specialized treatment. The eye may feel better but chemical penetration can continue.

👁️ Blunt Trauma / Suspected Perforation

DO NOT APPLY PRESSURE
  1. Protect the eye: Place a rigid eye shield or clean paper cup over the eye
  2. Secure carefully: Use gentle tape to keep shield in place without pressure
  3. DO NOT:
    • Apply direct pressure to the eye
    • Rub or massage the eye
    • Remove embedded foreign bodies
    • Apply eye drops or medications
  4. Transport immediately: Proceed directly to emergency with eye protected
  5. Fasting: Do not eat or drink (surgery may be needed)

⚠️ Tip: Blunt trauma can cause internal eye damage not immediately visible. Professional examination is essential.

⚡ Sudden Vision Loss

REQUIRES URGENT EXAMINATION
  1. Seek evaluation within 2 hours of symptom onset
  2. Symptoms requiring emergency care:
    • Sudden complete or partial vision loss
    • Sudden onset of flashing lights or floaters
    • Shadow or curtain over visual field
    • Sudden eye pain with vision changes
  3. Early intervention crucial for retinal detachment or vascular occlusions
  4. Do not delay: Call immediately and proceed to emergency

⚠️ Tip: Retinal emergencies have a narrow treatment window. Every minute counts.

✂️ Penetrating Foreign Bodies

DO NOT REMOVE
  1. Leave foreign body in place: Do not attempt removal at home
  2. Protect: Cover eye with rigid shield
  3. Immobilize: Keep head as still as possible during transport
  4. DO NOT:
    • Touch or attempt to remove the object
    • Apply pressure around it
    • Rinse or apply medications
  5. Emergency imaging: X-ray or ultrasound may be needed
  6. Urgent transport: Professional removal requires operating room

🔴 Hyphema (Blood in Anterior Chamber)

ELEVATION & REST REQUIRED
  1. Recognize: Blood visible inside eye after trauma
  2. Do not:
    • Apply pressure or massage
    • Engage in strenuous activity
    • Strain or exert
  3. Elevate: Keep head elevated above heart level
  4. Rest: Minimize eye movement and physical activity
  5. Monitor: Blood typically reabsorbs in days to weeks
  6. Seek care: Evaluation needed to exclude other injuries

👁️ Red Eye with Pain

SEEK IMMEDIATE EVALUATION

Redness combined with eye pain can indicate:

  • Corneal abrasion or ulcer
  • Acute glaucoma
  • Anterior uveitis
  • Traumatic iris injury

Do not wait — these conditions require urgent professional assessment.

What to Do When You Call 911 or Our Emergency Line

  1. State it's an eye emergency — this ensures trauma team readiness
  2. Describe the injury type (chemical, blunt, penetrating, etc.)
  3. Report first aid already given (e.g., irrigation time)
  4. Provide patient age (pediatric cases handled differently)
  5. Confirm transportation — ambulance, private vehicle, or rideshare
  6. Ask about bringing anything — relevant items, containers, or medications

Pre-Hospital Actions Summary

Injury Type DO DON'T
Chemical Burn Rinse 15–20 min with water Rub, delay, apply drops
Blunt Trauma Shield eye, keep still, fast Pressure, rub, open lids
Foreign Body Cover, don't touch, get help Remove, rinse, apply meds
Vision Loss Call immediately, describe Wait, delay, rub eyes

Why Speed Matters in Ocular Trauma

  • Chemical burns: Ongoing damage for hours after injury — continuous irrigation slows progression
  • Retinal detachment: 2-hour window for optimal visual recovery with emergency intervention
  • Globe rupture: Tissue loss accelerates with time — earlier repair preserves more vision
  • Vascular occlusion: Retinal artery occlusions have minutes, not hours, for treatment
  • Corneal scarring: Early professional management prevents vision-threatening scars

Remember: When in doubt, seek emergency evaluation. It's always better to be cautious with eye injuries — vision is irreplaceable.
